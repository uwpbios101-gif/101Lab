STEP 1 of 7 Pick one family — the other two fade out

There are three measurement families. Pick one. Everything else fades so you only see what you are working with.

STEP 2 of 7 Center row – the base unit itself

The center row of both tables is the base unit. It has no prefix and no exponent — it is the reference point everything else is measured against.

STEP 3 of 7 Bigger than the base – look ABOVE the center row, on the LEFT table

These prefixes make the unit larger than the base. Find the row that starts with 1 — then read right to see how many base units it equals.

STEP 4 of 7 Smaller than the base – look BELOW the center row, on the LEFT table

These prefixes make the unit smaller than the base. Find the row that starts with 1 — then read right to see how many base units it equals.

STEP 5 of 7 Find the exact “1” – not just the word

⚠️ Read this before you click anything.
When you scan the right table you will see the word meter (or gram/liter) on every row. That is the trap. You are looking for = 1 meter with nothing in between. If it says = 1×10³ meter, that is not 1 meter.
⚪ THE BASE "1" – look on the RIGHT table

Go to the right table. Find every row that says = 1 meter (or gram/liter) — nothing else in between.

STEP 6 of 7 See the pattern across all three families

The prefix pattern is identical for meters, grams, and liters. Highlight it to see for yourself.

STEP 7 of 7 Volume table – why is it shorter?

Volume tables are shortened because hekto‑, deka‑, deci‑, centi‑, nano‑, and angstrom‑liters are rarely used. But the prefix pattern still follows the same rule.

Every row on the left table starts with the number 1. That "1" tells you exactly what unit you have one of.

📍 Understanding the "1" — and where to find it in the table

Every row on the left table represents exactly one of something. The number 1 always appears at the very start of the row — before the unit name. Your job is simply to find the row whose name matches the unit you are working with. That's it. There is no trick.

⚪ Base unit — the "1" on the RIGHT table

The right table is where you physically see the number 1 written out on every single row — every row ends with "= 1 meter" (or gram, or liter). That right-hand column is always asking: "equals how many base units?" And the answer, for every row, is always 1.

Examples: = 1 meter (m)  ·  = 1 gram (g)  ·  = 1 liter (L)

👉 Go find it: click "Find 1 meter" — every "= 1 meter" cell in the right table lights up.

🟢 Above the base (larger units)

Rows above the center represent units with a prefix that makes them larger than the base. Each row still starts with 1 — for example, "1 kilometer (km)".

kilo- · hekto- · deka-

👉 Go find it: click "Find 1 kilogram" — watch the row light up above center.

🔴 Below the base (smaller units)

Rows below the center represent units with a prefix that makes them smaller than the base. Each row still starts with 1 — for example, "1 millimeter (mm)".

deci- · centi- · milli- · micro- · nano-

👉 Go find it: click "Find 1 nanometer" — watch how far below center it lands.

💡 You will always find the "1". The only question is which row to look at. If you know the unit name, scan the left column until you see "1 [that name]." The base unit is the only special case — it sits in the center row without a "1" because it is the "1" that everything else is compared to.
